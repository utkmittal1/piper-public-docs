A multi-step task is a job too big to finish in one sitting, such as researching a hundred accounts, auditing every page of a site, or working through a folder of contracts. Copus writes a plan, breaks the job into steps, runs them in rounds, and keeps going until the job is done.

## Start a multi-step task

<Steps>
  <Step title="Do the thinking in an ordinary task">
    Start a normal task and work out what you want. Gather the sources, settle the rules, and make any files the job will need. Copus carries everything you agree here into the multi-step task.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Select Switch to multi-step">
    Select **Switch to multi-step** in the top bar. You can select it once Copus finishes its reply. The **Switch to multi-step** button on a card Copus offers does the same thing. Copus switches the conversation to **Autopilot** at the same time, because a multi-step task works with nobody watching it. If the conversation has folders you added, Copus first asks you to confirm that the task can change files in them without asking.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Agree what done looks like">
    This conversation becomes the task's own conversation, and it keeps its title and every message and file already in it. Copus reads back through the conversation, asks about anything still unclear, and tells you what it's about to do. Answer Copus's questions, then tell Copus to go ahead.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Watch it work">
    Copus writes the plan and starts the first steps. You stay in the same conversation. The conversation's row stays in your **Tasks** list, now as a multi-step task. A task you started inside a project belongs to that project and appears in the project's list too.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
  You decide whether the work is big enough for a multi-step task. You can also ask Copus in a conversation to keep at a job until it is done. Copus then adds a card titled **Switch this to a multi-step task** to its reply, with a **Switch to multi-step** button. Nothing starts until you select that button, and typing yes doesn't start it. Selecting it switches the conversation exactly as the top-bar button does.
</Note>

<Note>
  Only an ordinary task can switch to multi-step. A task's own conversation, or a conversation inside a multi-step or scheduled task, can't become a task of its own, because a task can't hold another task. A review task and a question you ask in [Ask Copus](/working/describing-what-you-need#ask-copus-a-quick-question) can't become a multi-step task either. If you ask Copus for a multi-step task in Ask Copus or inside a task, Copus tells you why it can't and suggests starting from an ordinary conversation.
</Note>

## How Copus plans and hands out steps

An ordinary task is one conversation, described on [Tasks and the workspace](/concepts/sessions-and-workspace). A multi-step task holds many conversations. The conversation you started the work in becomes the conversation that plans the job, and Copus gives every step a conversation of its own.

That planning conversation is the task's own conversation. In it, Copus decides what finished looks like, writes the plan down, hands each piece of work out as a step, checks each step's result against that definition, and says when the job is done.

A step is a real piece of work one conversation can finish on its own, start to finish, whatever the other steps in its round do. Copus writes every step's result into the task's own folder, which outlives every conversation in the task.

Copus keeps the plan in a file called `plan.md`, in the workspace folder of the task's own conversation. Copus writes the plan before handing anything out and updates it every time work comes back, so the plan survives a conversation that gets cleared or restarted. To read it, select **Update task** on the task's page to open the task's own conversation.

### Rounds of steps

Copus hands work out in rounds. A round holds a limited number of steps, and every step in a round runs at the same time. Copus starts the next round only after every step in the round settles.

When the last step of a round settles, Copus posts in the task's own conversation how each step went: what the step was asked to do, what it reported, and one word for how it ended. That word is `delivered`, `failed`, `stopped, needs something`, `interrupted`, or `unreported`. Copus then updates the plan there and either opens the next round or says the job is finished. Copus repeats that cycle until the job is done.

Copus can carry on a step that failed, reported `Needs you`, was cut off, or ended unreported, instead of replacing it. Copus picks the step back up in its own conversation and its own folder, with everything the step already did. For a step that finished, or a step whose workspace Copus has already cleared away, Copus writes a fresh assignment instead.

<Note>
  In the task's own conversation, Copus labels each round's card as a batch, like `Batch 3`, with a badge showing how many steps the round holds. Copus caps the size of a round for two reasons. Copus runs a limited number of conversations at once. Steps that run together also can't see what the others decided, so a wider round leaves more steps working without each other's decisions.
</Note>

### Steps that end without reporting

A step that ends without saying how it went settles as `unreported`. Its card shows an **Unreported** badge and the line "This step ended without saying how it went." The step's reply and its folder are still there, so Copus settles the step later.

In the task's own conversation, Copus reads what the step produced and marks the step as delivered or failed, with one line on what the step produced. That line replaces the report on the step's card. Until Copus marks the step, the card keeps the **Unreported** badge. Copus can also carry the step on instead.

## What a step is given

Copus gives every step three things beyond what an ordinary task gets.

The first is its own assignment, which Copus writes in the task's own conversation and hands over as the first message in that step. Open the step to read it. The second is the task's **Instructions**, the rules every step follows, which you can read and edit yourself in the **Instructions** box on the task page. The third is a short snapshot of each of the most recent steps, with the path to that step's full work log, so a step can pick up what came before it. For a task inside a project, Copus also gives each step the project's instructions, sources, and memory, and tells it what the project's own conversations have been doing.

Copus keeps the task's own conversation, and every conversation you opened on the task, out of each step. Copus also withholds the tools for creating tasks from every step. Copus aims to keep the plan out of the instructions, so no step starts doing work that belongs to another step. Copus doesn't enforce that aim, so a plan written into the instructions reaches every step.

Steps in the same round start together, so each step works without seeing what the other steps are doing. That's why every assignment has to say where the step's result goes.

<Note>
  A step that's already running keeps the instructions it started with. Only steps created after a change, whether you made it or Copus did, follow the new instructions.
</Note>

<Note>
  Copus starts every step on **Autopilot** because nobody is watching, so Copus acts without stopping to ask. When a step can't get past a problem, Copus reports `Needs you` on the step and names what it needs, instead of guessing. A step can't email or invite anyone. When the work needs that, the step says so in its report. Copus also withholds the Chrome you're signed in to from every step, and refuses a credential file outright, because nobody is there to approve either. [Approvals and permissions](/control/approvals-and-permissions) covers both rules.
</Note>

## Follow the work and pause it

The task's page shows the whole job. To read Copus's reasoning, select **Update task** to open the task's own conversation.

The **Steps** tab shows its count, like `Steps · 12`, and lists every step on its own card. A card shows one of these labels when the step needs your attention:

- `Needs you` when the step stopped to ask you for something
- `Failed` when the step couldn't finish
- `Trying again due now` when Copus is about to restart the step, or `Retry held` while the task is paused
- `Unreported` when the step ended without saying how it went, until Copus settles it

On a step's second or third start, the card shows the attempt, like `Attempt 2 of 3`, while the step runs. Select a card to see what the step reported and the files it produced. To read everything the step did, select **Open step** on its card.

Select **Pause** in the header to stop the task. Copus stops each step running at that moment and marks it "Stopped when the task was paused." Select **Resume** to start the task again. Copus picks those steps back up before it plans anything new.

<Tip>
  The **Instructions** box on the task's page holds the rules every step follows. Select **Artifacts** in the top bar to see the task's own folder, with everything the job delivers and anything Copus keeps from one step to the next. For a task outside a project, Copus keeps the folder under `CopusData/projects` in your home folder. For a task inside a project, Copus keeps the folder in the project's Copus folder, under `tasks/`. Select **Memory** in the top bar for the facts Copus keeps for this task alone. See [Memory](/context/memory).
</Tip>

## Talk to a task while it runs

The **Conversations** tab lists the conversations you have opened on this task, with the count beside its name. Select **Start a conversation**, type your first message, and Copus opens the conversation inside the task and takes you into it.

Open a conversation of your own when you want to change the task without interrupting the work in the task's own conversation. From your own conversation, Copus can change the task's name, description, and instructions, rework the plan, and set the next round of steps going.

The task's own conversation is where Copus reports back. When a round of steps finishes, Copus writes the results there and plans the next round from the same place.

You can steer one task from several conversations at once. Copus gives each conversation the current plan and instructions, so a change you make in one conversation shows up in the others.

<Note>
  After you send a message in the task's own conversation, Copus waits before it asks there for new steps, so you have time to answer a question Copus asked you before it opens more steps.
</Note>

## Ask a finished task for more

Use **Resume** on the task page for a task you paused yourself. If you ask a paused task for more work, Copus tells you the task is paused and asks you to resume it there first.

## A task inside a project

Start a multi-step task from a conversation in a project and the task belongs to that project. Its row appears in the project's list. To get back to the project from the task's page, select **Back**, or open **Projects** in the sidebar.

A task inside a project follows the project's instructions and can use its shared files, its memory, and its sources, on top of its own. Copus keeps the task's folder inside the project and delivers the task's results there by default.

The task can write only to the project's Copus folder. For a project Copus manages, that's the whole project folder. For a project you pointed at a folder of your own, it's the subfolder Copus created there, so your own files around it stay out of reach. The task can also write in the folders it took from the conversation when the conversation switched to multi-step.

Only a multi-step task can sit inside a project. Copus always keeps a scheduled task outside any project.

<Warning>
  When you delete a project, Copus stops and deletes every multi-step task in it, with its steps and its folder.
</Warning>

## How a job finishes

Copus refuses to call a job finished in three cases: a step is still running, the task has never opened a step, or a step is still unreported. Copus says so in the task's own conversation and keeps working on what would actually finish the job. Past those checks, Copus decides when the work is done, with no further check. Read what the task produced before you rely on it.

## When a job gives up

A job stops before it's finished in two ways. Copus can say outright that the job can't go on and name what it needs from you. Copus then pauses the task.

Or Copus can stop opening steps. When nothing is running, Copus asks in the task's own conversation what comes next, and pauses the task after several passes in a row that open no step and declare nothing. Copus then tells you "It stopped saying what to do next, so nothing more is running. Resume it to carry on." Asking the task for more work also starts it again.

Copus notifies you when the whole job delivers and when it stops itself. Both notifications open with the task's own name, then "is done" or "stopped itself". Turn either off under **Settings**, then **Notifications**, where they are listed as **Job finished** and **Task stopped itself**. See [Notifications](/working/notifications).

<Warning>
  Copus moves a multi-step task forward only while Copus is open and your Mac is on and awake. Quitting Copus doesn't pause the task. Copus marks any step that was running as cut off, and picks it back up in the folder it was using the next time you open Copus.
</Warning>

<Note>
  Every restart of a step counts as one of its three attempts, whether Copus closed under it or you paused the task. Copus gives up on a step cut off three times. Select **Pause** before a long shutdown, and **Resume** when you're back.
</Note>
