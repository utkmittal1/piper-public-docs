A long-running task is a job too big to finish in one sitting: researching a hundred accounts, auditing every page of a site, working through a folder of contracts. Piper writes a plan, breaks the job into steps, runs them in rounds, and keeps going until the job is done.

## The Orchestrator and its steps

An ordinary task is one conversation, described on [Tasks and the workspace](/concepts/sessions-and-workspace). A long-running task is bigger than that. The conversation you started the work in becomes the one that plans it, and every step it hands out gets a conversation of its own.

Piper calls that planning conversation the **Orchestrator**, and it plans rather than works. It decides what finished looks like, writes the plan down, hands each piece out as a step, checks what comes back against its own bar, and says when the job is done.

A step is a real piece of work one conversation can finish on its own, start to finish, whatever the other steps in its round do. Every step writes its result into the task's own folder, which outlives every conversation in the task.

The plan itself lives in a file called `plan.md`, in the Orchestrator's own workspace folder. Piper writes it before handing anything out and updates it every time work comes back, so the plan survives a conversation that gets cleared or restarted. Open the Orchestrator to read it.

## What a step is given

Every step gets three things beyond what an ordinary task gets.

The first is its own assignment, which the Orchestrator writes and Piper hands over as the first message in that step. Open the step to read it. The second is the task's **Instructions**, the rules every step follows, which you can read and edit yourself in the rail on the task page. The third is a short snapshot of each of the ten most recent steps, with the path to that step's full work log, so a step can pick up what came before it. For a task inside a project, Piper also gives each step the project's instructions, sources, and memory, and tells it what the project's own conversations have been doing.

Piper keeps the plan out of the instructions on purpose, so no step starts doing work that belongs to another one, and it never gives a step the Orchestrator's conversation at all.

Steps in the same round start together, so none of them can see what the others are doing while they work. That is why every assignment has to say where the step's result goes. Left to themselves, five steps would each invent a different place.

<Note>
  A step already running never picks up a change you or the Orchestrator makes to the instructions afterwards. Only steps created after the change follow it.
</Note>

## Rounds of steps

Piper hands work out in rounds. A round holds at most five steps, and every step in a round runs at the same time. Nothing else starts while a step in the round is still going.

When the last step of a round settles, Piper tells the Orchestrator how each one went: what the step was asked to do, what it reported, and whether it delivered, failed, stopped needing something, or was cut off part-way. The Orchestrator then updates the plan and either opens the next round or says the job is finished. A step that stopped part-way can be carried on rather than replaced, so it picks back up in its own conversation and its own folder with everything it already did.

That cycle repeats. Plan, open a round, read the reports, open the next. A job of thirty steps runs as six rounds or more.

<Note>
  Five keeps a round small enough that the Orchestrator can read every result before it plans the next one, and a wider round does more work blind: steps running together cannot see what the others decided.
</Note>

<Note>
  Two words for the same thing. Piper calls each one a round, and the card it leaves in the **Orchestrator**'s conversation calls it `Batch 3`, with a badge saying how many steps it holds.
</Note>

<Note>
  Only a step that failed, that reported **Needs you**, or that was cut off can be carried on. A step that finished has nothing left to do, and a step whose conversation has been cleared away has nothing left to pick up from. Either way the Orchestrator writes a fresh assignment instead.
</Note>

## Start a long-running task

<Steps>
  <Step title="Do the thinking in an ordinary task">
    Start a normal task and work out what you want. Gather the sources, settle the rules, make any files the job will need. Everything you agree here goes across.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Select Run until done">
    The button sits in the task's header and reads **Run until done**. It is greyed out while the task is still answering you, so wait for the reply to finish. Piper switches the conversation to **Autopilot** at the same time, because a long-running task works with nobody watching it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Agree what done looks like">
    This conversation becomes the one that plans the work, and it keeps every message and every file already in it. Piper reads back through the conversation, asks about anything still unclear, and tells you what it is about to do. Answer its questions, then tell it to go ahead.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Watch it work">
    Piper writes the plan and starts the first steps. You stay in the same conversation. Its row stays in your **Tasks** list, now a long-running task. A task you started inside a project belongs to that project and appears in the project's list too.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
  Piper decides nothing about whether the work is big enough. You said it was when you selected **Run until done**.
</Note>

<Note>
  **Run until done** is not offered inside a long-running or scheduled task, or in the **Quick question** drawer. A task cannot hold another task.
</Note>

## The task page

<Frame>
  <img src="/images/diagrams/long-running-task.svg" alt="One large job at the top. Piper's planning conversation splits it into a round of steps that run side by side, each in its own conversation. The steps report back, and the results rejoin the plan, which hands out the next round." />
</Frame>

Everything about the job sits on the task's page. Read the **Orchestrator** panel at the top for what it is doing: `Working` while it is thinking, `Waiting on 3 steps` while a round runs, or `Idle` with how long it has been. Under that sits the opening line of whatever it last said. Select the panel to open the conversation and read the reasoning yourself.

Below that, the **Steps** tab carries the count in its own name, so it reads `Steps · 12`, and lists every step on its own card. Each card carries its own status: `Queued`, `Working`, `Done`, `Needs you`, `Failed`, or `Trying again`, and a step on its second or third try adds `Attempt 2 of 3` while it runs. Expand a card to read what the step reported, see anything it was blocked on, and open the files it produced. **Open conversation** on the card opens the step itself.

The badge beside the task's name reads **Working** whenever the job is live, and the line under it shows how far it has got: `3 steps going` while a round runs, `12 steps done` between rounds, or `not started yet` before the first one. There is no percentage. Piper counts the steps it has actually opened and finished, and the plan keeps changing as the work teaches the Orchestrator something, so there is no fixed total to count toward.

Select **Pause** in the header to stop the task. Piper stops each step running at that moment and marks it "Stopped when the task was paused." Select **Resume** to start it again, and Piper picks those steps back up before it asks the Orchestrator for anything new.

<Tip>
  The rail on the right of the task page holds three panels. **Instructions** are the rules every step follows. **Artifacts** is the task's own folder, which holds everything the job delivers and anything it keeps from one step to the next. A task that stands alone keeps its folder under `PiperData` in your home folder. A task inside a project keeps it inside that project's folder. **Memory** holds the facts Piper keeps for this task alone. See [Memory](/context/memory).
</Tip>

## Talking to a task while it runs

The **Conversations** tab lists the conversations you have opened on this task, and holds one button, **Start a conversation**. The **Orchestrator** is not among them; it has its own panel at the top of the page. Select it, type your first message, and Piper opens the conversation inside the task and takes you into it.

Open one when you want to change the task without interrupting the conversation that is running it. Such a conversation can change the task's name, description, and instructions, rework the plan, and set the next round of steps going.

The **Orchestrator** is where Piper reports back. When a round of steps finishes, Piper writes the results there and plans the next round from the same place.

Two conversations can steer one task at once. Each sees the current plan and instructions, so a change you make in one shows up in the other.

## Asking a finished task for more

Open the **Orchestrator** of a finished task, or start a conversation from the **Conversations** tab, and ask for more work. Piper plans it, opens the steps, and the task is working again. The same holds for a task that stopped itself, whether it stopped saying what to do next or reported that it could not go on.

**Resume** on the task page is for a task you paused yourself. Ask one of those for more work and it tells you the task is paused and to resume it there first.

<Note>
  Piper leaves the conversation alone for five minutes after your last message, so a question it asked you has time to get an answer before it opens more steps.
</Note>

## A task inside a project

Start a long-running task from a conversation in a project and the task belongs to that project. Its row appears in the project's list, and the project's name sits above the title on the task's page. Select it to go back to the project.

A task inside a project follows the project's instructions and can use its shared files, its memory, and its sources, on top of its own. It keeps its own folder inside the project's folder and delivers there by default, so the project's shared files change only when you ask the task to change them.

Only a long-running task can sit inside a project. A scheduled task always stands on its own.

Deleting the project deletes its long-running tasks with it.

## How a job finishes

Piper refuses to call a job finished while a step is still running, or while the task has never opened a step at all. It says so in the **Orchestrator**'s conversation and asks for the steps that would actually finish the job. Beyond those two checks, "done" is the Orchestrator's own judgment about the work, not a guarantee it is good. Read what it produced.

## When a job gives up

A job can stop itself two ways. The Orchestrator can say outright that it cannot go on and name what it needs from you, and Piper takes that at its word. Or it can go quiet, and Piper pauses the task after waking it three times without it opening a step or declaring anything, then tells you "It stopped saying what to do next, so nothing more is running. Resume it to carry on." Asking it for more work starts it again too.

Steps have their own give-up rules, and Piper applies all three. It starts a step at most three times, counting the first. It gives up on a step still unfinished a day after that step first started, because finishing yesterday's work today is worse than not finishing it. And it cancels a step that writes nothing, says nothing, and calls no tool for twenty minutes, so the round that step belongs to can settle rather than waiting on it forever. In every case the round moves on.

Piper notifies you when the whole job delivers and when it stops itself. Both notifications open with the task's own name, then "is done" or "stopped itself". Turn either off under **Settings** > **Notifications**, where they are listed as **Job finished** and **Task stopped itself**. See [Notifications](/working/notifications).

<Warning>
  A long-running task only moves while Piper is open and your Mac is on and awake. Quitting Piper does not pause the task. Piper marks any step that was running as cut off, and picks it back up in the folder it was using next time you open Piper.
</Warning>

<Note>
  Every restart of a step counts as one of its three attempts, whether Piper closed under it or you paused the task. Piper gives up on a step cut off three times. Select **Pause** before a long shutdown, and **Resume** when you are back.
</Note>

<Note>
  Steps run with nobody watching, so they start on **Autopilot** and act without stopping to ask. A step that cannot get past something reports **Needs you** and names what it needs, rather than guessing. Nobody is there to answer a step's question. So Piper never uses the Chrome you are signed in to in a step, and refuses a credential file at once instead of waiting ten minutes. [Approvals and permissions](/control/approvals-and-permissions) covers both modes.
</Note>
